How to use a garden calculator without wasting money

A calculator is only as good as the measurements you put into it. Measure the inside of the bed, not the outside. Measure depth in inches, not feet. Count the area you are actually covering, not the whole garden. Then round up for soil and mulch — fresh material settles after watering, and a half-empty bed is worse than a bag left over for topping up.

For fertilizer, a calculator cannot replace a soil test. It can convert a recommendation into product weight, but it cannot tell you whether you need that recommendation at all. For seed, germination is a range, not a promise — old seed, cold soil and deep sowing all lower it, so sow a buffer and thin to final spacing.

Common mistakes calculators cannot catch

  • Mulch volcanoes: piling mulch against tree trunks or plant stems traps moisture and invites rot. Keep mulch a few inches clear.
  • Shallow watering: daily light watering keeps roots shallow. Water deeply, then let the top inch dry before watering again.
  • Planting into cold soil: tomatoes and other warm-season crops stall below about 60°F (15°C) soil temperature, even after the last frost.
  • Forgetting paths: a bed you cannot reach will not be harvested. Leave access, even if the spacing calculator says you could fit more plants.
  • Buying soil by the bag without checking volume: bags vary (1, 1.5, 2 cubic feet are common). Always convert to cubic feet first.

Frequently asked questions

How much soil do I need for a raised bed?

Multiply length (ft) × width (ft) × depth (in) ÷ 12 for cubic feet, then ÷ 27 for cubic yards. Use the soil volume calculator and buy about 10% extra for settling.

How deep should mulch be?

2–3 inches for most beds, 3–4 inches for paths. More than 4 inches in a planting bed can stay too wet. See the mulch depth guide.

How often should I water vegetables?

Most vegetables need about 1 inch per week from rain plus watering, but sandy soil dries faster and clay holds water longer. Check soil moisture at 2 inches deep before watering on a schedule. See watering by soil type.

When should I start seeds indoors?

Count back from your last frost date by the weeks listed on the seed packet, usually 4–8 weeks. Use the seed starting calendar and harden seedlings off before transplanting.

Do I need a soil test?

If you are fertilizing heavily, seeing consistent poor growth, or starting a new bed, yes. A test tells you pH, organic matter and nutrients for your soil — a generic chart cannot. See soil testing basics.

How our calculators work

Every calculator on this site is built around a simple idea: give a precise answer in under a minute, and show how that answer was reached so you can trust it. Each tool uses real-world inputs — actual bag sizes in cubic feet, the depth you actually plan to pour or spread, the rate on the seed packet or fertilizer label — not generic averages. Where a number varies by product, such as concrete bag yield or gravel weight per yard, the page says so and tells you to check the label or supplier, rather than presenting a guess as a fact.

  • Imperial units first: feet, inches, gallons and pounds, because that is how US seed packets, fertilizer labels and supplier quotes are written. Metric conversions are shown where they help.
  • Real bag and bulk sizes: results in cubic feet, cubic yards and bag counts, so you can compare bagged and bulk prices directly.
  • Formulas shown: every tool lists its formula and a worked example. The full list is on the Methodology page.
  • Reviewed, not guessed: each page is checked against a hand-calculated example, and shows its last review date at the bottom.
