Succession Planting

Succession planting: sow in small batches every 1–3 weeks for a steady harvest instead of one glut.

Small batches, steady harvest

Sow fast crops (lettuce, radish, beans) in small batches every 1–3 weeks rather than all at once. When one batch finishes, replant the space with a different family if you can, to reduce pest and disease build-up. Keep a simple notebook of sowing dates — that record is worth more than any app for next year. Use the seed quantity calculator per batch, not for the whole season at once.

How succession planting works

Succession planting means sowing or transplanting the same crop at intervals — typically every 2–3 weeks — instead of all at once, so harvests are spread over the season. It also means replacing a finished crop with a different one in the same space: peas followed by beans, or spring lettuce followed by autumn brassicas. Both approaches use the same bed more efficiently and avoid the feast-or-famine pattern of a single planting.

A simple succession plan

  • Early spring: peas, lettuce, radish and spinach — fast, cold-tolerant crops that finish before summer heat.
  • Late spring: beans, courgettes and tomatoes take over as the early crops finish.
  • Mid-summer: sow a second round of lettuce, beans and carrots for late summer and autumn harvest.
  • Late summer: sow autumn and winter crops — brassicas, leeks and overwintering onions — in space freed by summer crops.

Planning tips

Keep a simple notebook of sowing dates and harvest dates. After one season you will know your real intervals, which are more useful than any generic chart. Leave a few days between clearing a crop and replanting, to add compost and let the soil recover. Do not follow a crop with another from the same family in the same spot if you can avoid it — rotation reduces pest and disease build-up. Use the seed quantity calculator for each sowing, not for the whole season at once.

Frequently asked questions

Does succession work for all crops?

Best for fast, repeat-harvest crops: lettuce, radish, beans, carrots, courgettes. Slow, single-harvest crops like pumpkins and winter squash are usually planted once.

What if I run out of space?

Interplant fast crops between slow ones — radish between carrots, lettuce under tomatoes before they shade the bed. The fast crop is harvested before the slow one needs the space.

More questions

What is the easiest succession crop to start with?

Lettuce and radish — they grow fast, so a 2–3 week gap between sowings is easy to see working, and a mistake costs little.

Do I need to fertilize between successions?

A light top-up of compost between crops helps, especially after a heavy feeder such as tomatoes or brassicas. Do not replant the same family in the same spot if you can avoid it.
