7 Raised Bed Mistakes Beginners Make (and How to Fix Them)

Raised beds make gardening easier — until a small mistake costs you a season. These seven are the ones I see most often, and every one is fixable.

5 November 2026 · Garden Calculator Pro

1. Building the bed too shallow

Most vegetables need at least 12 inches of soil, and root crops need 18 inches or more. A 6-inch bed dries out fast, heats up in summer, and leaves carrots and parsnips with nowhere to go. If your bed is already shallow, add a second board on top and fill it — it is cheaper than rebuilding.

2. Filling it with garden soil alone

Garden soil on its own compacts in a raised bed, drains poorly, and can carry weed seeds. Mix it: roughly 60% topsoil, 30% compost and 10% aeration material such as perlite or coarse grit. Use the raised bed soil mix calculator to work out the amounts for your bed size.

3. Forgetting drainage

A raised bed on concrete or compacted clay holds water like a bathtub. Loosen the ground underneath before you build, and never line the bottom with plastic. Landscape fabric is fine — it lets water through while keeping soil in.

4. Making the bed too wide to reach

If you cannot reach the middle without stepping in, the bed is too wide. Four feet is the maximum for a bed you reach from both sides, two feet if you only reach it from one side. Stepping into a bed compacts the soil you just built.

5. Skipping mulch

Bare soil in a raised bed dries out in a day in summer. A 2–3 inch mulch layer keeps moisture in, suppresses weeds and feeds soil life as it breaks down. Work out how much you need with the mulch calculator.

6. Planting everything at once

Sowing a whole bed on the same day gives you a glut, then nothing. Sow salad leaves and beans every two to three weeks instead — small, regular sowings beat one big one. The succession planting guide explains the rhythm.

7. Never testing the soil

If plants look poor year after year, guesswork is expensive. A lab soil test every 2–3 years costs less than a season of fertilizer you did not need, and it tells you the one number that matters most: pH. Read the soil testing guide before you buy anything.

Choosing the right location: sun, water and drainage

Most raised bed mistakes happen before a single seed goes in, and location is the first of them. Vegetables need 6–8 hours of direct sun a day — one hour less can visibly cut yields, so watch your yard for a full day before you build and pick the sunniest spot, even if it is not the prettiest corner. Keep the bed within reach of a hose, because a bed you cannot water easily is a bed you will water less. And avoid low spots where water pools after rain: a raised bed helps drainage, but it does not fix a swamp. Level the ground before you build, because an unlevel bed sends water to one side, leaves the other side dry, and pushes soil against the low wall until it bows.

Raised bed soil mix that actually works

The most expensive raised bed mistake is treating soil like filler. Garden soil alone compacts, drains badly and chokes roots; plain topsoil from a random source is often no better; and pure compost holds too much water and has no structure. A mix that works for most vegetables is about 60% topsoil, 30% compost and 10% aeration material such as perlite or coarse grit — use the raised bed soil mix calculator to get the exact volumes for your bed. Never use fresh manure in a mix — it is too strong and can carry pathogens — and skip landscape fabric at the bottom, which blocks roots and traps water; cardboard smothers grass and breaks down instead. For a deep bed, you do not need premium mix all the way down: fill the bottom with logs, branches and leaves in the hugelkultur style, then put the good mix in the top 12 inches where the roots actually live.

How wide and how deep: the numbers that matter

Width is a reach problem, not a size problem. A bed wider than 4 feet cannot be reached from both sides without stepping in, and stepping in compacts the soil you just built — every footprint undoes your work. Two feet is the maximum if you only reach it from one side, against a wall or fence. Depth is a cost problem: 12 inches suits most vegetables, 18 inches suits carrots, parsnips and potatoes, and anything deeper mostly costs more soil without growing more food. A bed that is too tall also dries out faster and can wobble in wind. If your bed is already too shallow, add another board on top and fill it rather than rebuilding from scratch.

Watering raised beds without drowning them

Raised beds drain faster than in-ground soil, so they need water more often — but watering a little every day trains roots to stay shallow. Water deeply and less often, so moisture reaches the full root zone, and check with your finger: if the top 2 inches are dry but it is moist below, the bed is fine. Mulch is what makes this work — a 2–3 inch layer cuts evaporation, keeps soil temperature steady and suppresses weeds, and the mulch calculator tells you how much to buy. Overwatering is just as common as underwatering: soggy soil drowns roots and invites disease, so if water sits on the surface minutes after watering, stop and let it drain. Drip lines or soaker hoses beat sprinklers here, because they water the soil, not the leaves, and they do it slowly enough to soak in.

Planning before planting

Planting without a plan is how a bed ends up with tomatoes shading lettuce, beans with nowhere to climb, and everything sown on the same day. Group plants by their needs — heat lovers together, cool-season crops together — and give each one its packet spacing, because overcrowding cuts airflow, invites mildew and starves plants of light. Rotate crops each year so the same family does not sit in the same soil, which builds up pests and disease; tomatoes, peppers and potatoes are one family, brassicas another. And stagger your sowings: salad leaves and beans every two to three weeks give you a continuous harvest instead of a glut followed by nothing, as the succession planting guide explains.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should a raised bed be?

At least 12 inches for most vegetables, 18 inches or more for root crops. Shallower beds dry out faster and limit root growth.

Can I build a raised bed on grass?

Yes — lay cardboard over the grass, build the bed on top, and fill it. The cardboard smothers the grass and breaks down over the season.

Do raised beds need replacing?

Timber beds last 5–10 years depending on the wood and climate. The soil inside, topped up with compost each year, lasts indefinitely.

How much soil do I need for a raised bed?

Multiply length × width × depth and divide by 12 for cubic feet — an 8 × 4 ft bed at 12 inches deep needs 32 cubic feet. Use the soil volume calculator to get the exact number, including bag count, before you order.

Can I use only compost to fill a raised bed?

No. Pure compost holds too much water, has no structure, and settles dramatically as it breaks down. Mix it with topsoil and aeration material — about 30% compost in the total mix is right for most beds.
