Fertilizer Calculator (NPK)

Convert a nitrogen recommendation in lb N per 1,000 sq ft into product weight for your fertilizer grade. Uses the first (N) number only.

Fertilizer Calculator

Formula

Product (lb) = (nitrogen needed × area ÷ 1,000) ÷ (N% ÷ 100). Example: 1 lb N per 1,000 sq ft over 1,000 sq ft with 10-10-10 (10% N) needs 10 lb of product.

Safety notes

Do not guess a nitrogen target from this page alone — use a soil test or local extension recommendation for your crop and soil. Water after applying granular fertilizer, and never apply to dry, stressed plants at full rate without checking the product label.

How to use it, step by step

Start with a recommendation, ideally from a soil test, in pounds of nitrogen (N) per 1,000 square feet. Enter your area in square feet and the first number of your fertilizer's NPK label — for a 10-10-10 fertilizer, that is 10. The calculator uses only the nitrogen number, because most recommendations are given as nitrogen. It returns the weight of product to spread. Spread it evenly, water it in, and do not double up because the result looks small.

Worked example in detail

Suppose the recommendation is 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet, your bed is 500 square feet, and your fertilizer is 10-10-10. Product needed = (1 × 500 ÷ 1,000) ÷ (10 ÷ 100) = 5 lb. With a 20-0-0 fertilizer, the same nitrogen needs only 2.5 lb of product, because it is twice as concentrated. That is why the NPK numbers matter more than the bag size.

What affects the right amount

Soil test results, crop type, previous fertilizing, compost already added, and soil type all change the right amount. Compost adds nutrients slowly and is not counted in this calculator. Sandy soil leaches nitrogen faster than clay, so small, frequent applications suit it better than one large dose. Over-fertilizing burns roots and pollutes runoff, so a soil test is worth more than a bigger bag.

Frequently asked questions

Why only the first NPK number?

Because recommendations are usually given as nitrogen, and nitrogen drives leaf growth. Phosphorus and potassium are supplied in proportion by a balanced fertilizer, but if your soil test shows a specific shortage of one of them, follow the lab's advice for that nutrient instead.

Can I use this for liquid fertilizer?

The maths is the same, but you must convert weight to volume using the product's density or its label dilution. Do not guess — follow the label for liquids.

What if I already added compost?

Compost is a slow, low-strength nutrient source. It does not replace a fertilizer recommendation in the same season, but over years it reduces how much fertilizer a bed needs. Retest soil every few years to see the trend.
