Drip Irrigation Calculator

Calculate weekly water use for a drip system from emitter count, flow rate in gallons per hour, and hours run per week.

Drip Irrigation Calculator

Formula

Gallons per week = emitters × flow rate (gal/hr) × hours per week. Example: 20 emitters at 1 gal/hr for 2 hours per week use 40 gallons per week.

How to use it, step by step

Count emitters, not plants — a large plant may have two emitters, a small plant one. Find the flow rate on the emitter (common rates are 0.5, 1 and 2 gallons per hour) or on its packaging. Enter total hours run per week, adding up all watering sessions. The result is total system use; divide by the number of plants for a per-plant figure, and compare it with the watering calculator target for your bed area.

Worked example in detail

A bed with 20 emitters at 1 gal/hr, run for 1 hour twice a week (2 hours total), uses 40 gallons per week. If the bed is 100 square feet needing 1 inch per week, the target is about 62 gallons — so this schedule under-waters by about a third, and either more hours or more emitters are needed. That comparison is the point of the calculator: drip hours alone do not tell you whether plants get enough water.

What affects drip watering in practice

Soil type matters most: sandy soil needs shorter, more frequent runs, because water drains down in a narrow column; clay needs longer, slower runs, because water spreads sideways but soaks in slowly. Emitter clogging from hard water or sediment reduces real flow below the rated flow, so check emitters seasonally. Mulch over drip lines reduces evaporation and keeps the wetting pattern where roots are. In summer, check soil moisture at root depth, not just the surface, before increasing hours.

Frequently asked questions

How many emitters per plant?

One for small plants, two for shrubs and tomatoes, spaced around the root zone rather than at the stem. More emitters spread water wider, which encourages wider root growth.

Is drip always better than sprinklers?

For beds and rows, usually yes — less evaporation, dry leaves, and water only where plants are. For lawns, sprinklers are more practical. Drip is not a set-and-forget system: filters and emitters need checking.

Why is my soil dry between emitters?

Because drip wets a limited zone. In sandy soil the zone is narrow and deep; in clay it is wide and shallow. Add emitters or increase run time, and mulch to help the wet zones join up.
