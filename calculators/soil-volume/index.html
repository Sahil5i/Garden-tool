Soil Volume Calculator — Raised Bed

Calculate raised bed soil volume in cubic feet and cubic yards, plus bags needed. Formula: length × width × depth ÷ 12, then ÷ 27 for cubic yards.

Soil Volume Calculator

Formula

Cubic feet = length (ft) × width (ft) × depth (in) ÷ 12. Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27. Example: an 8 ft × 4 ft bed at 12 in deep needs 8 × 4 × 12 ÷ 12 = 32 cubic feet, or 1.19 cubic yards.

Common mistakes

  • Entering depth in feet instead of inches — that makes the result 12× too small.
  • Forgetting settling: fresh soil settles after watering. Buy about 10% extra, or top up after the first watering.
  • Using the outside dimensions of a bed with thick walls — measure the inside.

How to use it, step by step

First measure the inside length and inside width of the bed in feet. If the bed is not a rectangle, split it into rectangles, calculate each one, and add them together. Next measure depth in inches from the top of the bed to where the soil will sit, not to the ground below if the bed is open-bottomed and you plan to fill only part of it. Enter bag size only if you are buying bagged soil; for bulk delivery, use the cubic yards figure. Finally, add about 10% for settling and spillage, and round up to whole bags or a sensible delivery quantity.

Worked example in detail

Take a common 8 ft by 4 ft raised bed, 12 inches deep. Cubic feet = 8 × 4 × 12 ÷ 12 = 32. Cubic yards = 32 ÷ 27 = 1.19. In 2-cubic-foot bags that is 16 bags before the settling allowance, and 18 bags with 10% added. If the same bed were only 6 inches deep, the volume halves to 16 cubic feet, or 0.59 cubic yards — depth matters as much as length and width, which is why entering it in the wrong unit is so costly.

What affects the real amount you need

Fresh soil and compost settle noticeably after the first few waterings, often by an inch or more in a deep bed. Soil already in the bed, large stones, and buried logs in a hugelkultur-style bed all reduce the volume you need to buy. Conversely, soil that is very loose when delivered will compact more. The practical approach is to fill, water well, let it settle for a day, and then top up to the level you want before planting, rather than trying to buy an exact volume on the first order.

Frequently asked questions

Should I buy bags or bulk soil?

For one small bed, bags are convenient and easy to store. For several beds, bulk delivery by the cubic yard is usually much cheaper per cubic foot, but check the delivery charge and whether the truck can reach your garden.

What if my bed is round?

Use the container soil approach: volume = 3.14 × radius² × height, with radius and height in the same unit, then convert to cubic feet. A round bed 4 ft across and 12 inches deep holds about 12.6 cubic feet.

Can I fill the bottom with something else?

Yes, but only with material that will break down and not block drainage. Logs, branches and straw are used in hugelkultur beds; avoid plastic, rubble and treated wood, which do not belong in a growing bed.

Understanding your results

The result gives three numbers for the same volume: cubic feet (useful for bags), cubic yards (useful for bulk delivery) and bag count. If the bag count looks high, check the bag size you entered — a 1-cubic-foot bag needs twice as many bags as a 2-cubic-foot bag for the same bed. Use the cubic yards figure when comparing bulk suppliers, and the bag count when buying from a garden centre.

How much extra soil should I actually buy?

About 10% extra for settling and spillage. Fresh soil settles by an inch or more after the first waterings, so a bed filled exactly to the top will soon sit low. Top up after the first watering rather than overfilling at the start.

Does the calculator count the bed walls?

No — measure the inside dimensions, not the outside. Thick timber or brick walls can take a surprising amount of volume if you measure outside them.

What if my bed is not level?

Measure depth at the shallowest point and the deepest point and use the average. A bed on a slope needs more soil on the downhill side, and the average is the honest figure to order by.
