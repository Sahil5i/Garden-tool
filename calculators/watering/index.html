Watering Calculator

Calculate gallons and litres needed to apply inches of water over an area. 1 inch over 1 sq ft = 0.623 gallons.

Watering Calculator

Formula

Gallons = area (sq ft) × inches × 0.623. Litres = gallons × 3.785. Example: 1 inch over 32 sq ft = about 20 gallons (75 litres).

Most vegetables need about 1 inch per week from rain plus watering, but sandy soil dries faster and clay holds water longer — check soil moisture with a finger test before watering on a schedule.

How to use it, step by step

Enter the area in square feet and the depth of water you want in inches. One inch per week, from rain plus watering, is a common starting point for vegetables. The calculator converts to gallons (area × inches × 0.623) and litres. If it rained 0.5 inches this week, water only the remaining 0.5 inches. Check soil moisture before watering on a fixed schedule — the calendar does not know your soil.

Worked example in detail

A 100-square-foot bed needing 1 inch of water requires 100 × 1 × 0.623 = 62.3 gallons, or about 236 litres. A 4 ft by 8 ft bed (32 square feet) at the same depth needs about 20 gallons. Those numbers surprise many gardeners, and they explain why a quick sprinkle from a watering can does not count as watering — it may deliver only a fraction of an inch.

What affects how much water a bed really needs

Sandy soil drains fast and needs more frequent watering; clay holds water longer but can stay soggy. Mulch reduces evaporation. Wind, heat and plant size all increase water use, while shade and rain reduce it. New transplants need frequent watering until roots establish, while established plants do better with deep, less frequent watering that encourages deep roots.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to water morning or evening?

Morning is best: leaves dry during the day, which reduces disease, and less water evaporates than at midday. Evening watering is acceptable if morning is impossible, but wet leaves overnight invite fungal problems.

How do I know if I watered enough?

Push a finger or a trowel 2–3 inches into the soil after watering. If it is moist at that depth, you watered enough. If only the surface is wet, water longer, not more often.

Do I count rain?

Yes. A rain gauge, or a straight-sided container left in the open, tells you how much rain fell. Subtract it from the weekly target before watering.

Understanding your results

The result is the total water for the area and depth you entered — a large number, because 1 inch of water over a whole bed is a lot of water. Compare it with what you actually apply: a quick sprinkle from a can is usually only a fraction of an inch. If your usual watering is far below the result, your plants have been getting less water than you thought.

How do I measure inches of water in practice?

Use a rain gauge, or a straight-sided container left in the open during watering. If it collects 0.5 inches in your usual watering session, you need two sessions to apply 1 inch.

Should I water the whole bed or just the plants?

Just the plants, where possible. Watering paths and bare soil wastes water and encourages weeds. Drip lines and soaker hoses do this better than sprinklers.

Does the time of day change how much water I need?

Not the total, but morning watering loses less to evaporation than midday watering, so more of what you apply reaches the roots. The calculator's total is the same either way.
