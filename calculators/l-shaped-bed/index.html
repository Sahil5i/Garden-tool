L-Shaped Raised Bed Calculator

Calculate soil volume for an L-shaped raised bed from its two rectangles and depth, without double-counting the overlap.

L-Shaped Raised Bed Calculator

Formula

Cubic feet = (main length × main width + wing length × wing width) × depth (in) ÷ 12; cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27. Example: an 8×4 ft main section plus a 4×4 ft wing at 12 in deep needs (32 + 16) × 12 ÷ 12 = 48 cubic feet, or 1.78 cubic yards.

How to use it, step by step

Split the L into two rectangles: the main rectangle, and the wing that sticks out beyond it. Measure the wing only as the part beyond the main rectangle — if you measure the wing's full length including the corner overlap, you will double-count that corner and over-order soil. Enter depth in inches, then use the soil mix calculator to split the total into topsoil, compost and aeration material.

Worked example in detail

Take a bed shaped like an L: main section 8 ft × 4 ft, wing 4 ft × 4 ft beyond the main section, depth 12 inches. Main area = 32 sq ft, wing area = 16 sq ft, total 48 sq ft. At 12 inches deep, volume = 48 cubic feet. If you had measured the wing as 8 ft long (including the 4 ft corner overlap), you would have calculated 64 sq ft and 64 cubic feet — 16 cubic feet too much, or about 8 extra bags.

What affects real soil needs

The same settling rule as any raised bed applies: fresh soil settles after watering, so add about 10% or top up after the first watering. Corners are harder to fill evenly than straight sections, so check corners when filling and top them up separately. An L-shaped bed also has more edge than a rectangle of the same area, so it dries out faster at the edges — mulch helps, especially in the wing.

Frequently asked questions

What if my bed is U-shaped?

Split it into three rectangles — two wings plus the base — and add their areas, using the same no-overlap rule for each join.

Can I use this for a rectangular bed?

Yes — set the wing to zero. But the rectangular soil volume calculator is simpler for that job.

Should I build an L-shaped bed at all?

L-shaped beds fit corners and awkward spaces well, and give more edge access than a long rectangle. They are harder to mow around and to cover with a single cold frame, so weigh fit against those practical points.

Understanding your results

The result is the total soil volume for both rectangles of the L, without double-counting the corner. If the result looks too large, check the wing measurement — the most common error is measuring the wing's full length including the corner overlap. The wing should be only the part that sticks out beyond the main rectangle.

Can I use this calculator for a U-shaped bed?

Yes, by splitting it into three rectangles — two wings plus the base — and adding their areas, using the same no-overlap rule at each join. Calculate each rectangle separately and add them.

Is an L-shaped bed harder to water?

Slightly — corners dry out faster than straight sections, because they have more edge. Mulch helps, and check corners first when deciding whether to water.

What should I grow in the wing?

Use the wing for crops that need different care, such as herbs that like drier soil, while the main section holds thirstier vegetables. The shape makes it easy to treat the two parts differently.
